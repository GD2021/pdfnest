How to Save a Webpage as a PDF

PBy the PDFNest Team·November 10, 2026·5 min read

Booking confirmations, receipts, articles and online statements have a habit of disappearing once you close the tab. Saving the page as a PDF keeps a copy that looks the same on any device and opens without an internet connection. Every browser can already do it — the skill is in getting a clean result rather than a PDF full of cookie banners, cut-off text and blank image boxes.

Prepare the page before saving

A few seconds of preparation make the biggest difference. Close cookie banners, newsletter pop-ups and chat bubbles first — anything floating over the page will be captured in the PDF, often covering the text you wanted.

Then scroll slowly to the bottom of the page. Many sites only load images, comments and later sections as you reach them, so a page saved straight after opening can come out with blank boxes or end halfway. Scrolling forces everything to load. Expand any collapsed sections you need, such as “show more” blocks or order details.

If the browser offers a reader view — a simplified, text-focused version of the page — consider saving from that instead for articles and long reads. It strips menus, adverts and sidebars, and usually produces a shorter, cleaner PDF. For receipts and statements, keep the normal view so tables and totals appear as the site laid them out.

Save as PDF in Chrome, Edge and Firefox

Press Ctrl+P on Windows or Command+P on a Mac to open the print dialog. In Chrome and Edge, set the destination or printer to “Save as PDF”; in Firefox, choose “Save to PDF”. Then click Save and choose where the file goes. Nothing is printed and no paper is involved.

Before saving, open the extra settings. Choose the paper size you want — A4 or US Letter — and turn on background graphics if coloured boxes, highlights or images are missing from the preview. Adjusting the scale or setting the margins to minimum can stop wide tables being cut off at the right-hand edge.

The resulting PDF usually keeps the text selectable and searchable, and in Chrome and Edge the links stay clickable. If the preview shows only part of a long page, or the layout looks broken, try a different paper size or orientation — landscape often helps wide pages.

Save as PDF in Safari on a Mac

In Safari, open the File menu and choose Export as PDF, then pick a name and location. This is the quickest route and produces a PDF of the page as you see it.

For more control, use Print from the File menu instead. The print dialog lets you choose the paper size, scale and orientation and preview the result, and its PDF menu at the bottom includes Save as PDF. Use this route when a page comes out cut off or squeezed with the quick export.

Safari’s Reader view, available from the address bar on many article pages, is worth using before saving long articles. Saving from Reader produces a tidy, readable PDF without the site’s navigation and advertising.

Save a webpage as a PDF on your phone

On an iPhone or iPad, take a screenshot in Safari, tap the preview, and choose Full Page at the top. That captures the entire page, not just the visible screen, and you can save it as a PDF to the Files app. The share sheet’s options can also save the page as a PDF, depending on your iOS version.

On Android, open the page in Chrome, tap the menu, choose Share and then Print, and pick “Save as PDF” as the printer. Tap the download button and choose a location. The same tips apply as on a computer: close pop-ups and scroll through the page first.

Phone-saved PDFs can be large when a page is long and full of images. If you need to email or upload one, Compress PDF can reduce it in your phone’s browser — the guide to compressing a PDF on your phone covers the steps.

Convert a saved page or HTML code instead

Sometimes you do not have the live page any more — only a saved copy, an exported report or a block of HTML code. That is where a converter helps. Open HTML to PDF, add the .html file or paste the code, choose A4 or Letter, orientation and margins, and convert. For files ending in .htm, HTM to PDF does the same.

The tool prepares the page in your browser and opens the print dialog, where you choose “Save as PDF” — so the result is a real, text-selectable PDF, and nothing is uploaded. It cannot fetch a page from a web address: it works on files and code you already have. For a live page, the browser’s own Save as PDF above is the right tool.

Saved pages often depend on a folder of images stored next to the file. Keep that folder in place when converting, or the images will be missing. The guide to HTM files explains why that happens and how to fix it.

Check the PDF before you rely on it

Open the saved PDF and scroll to the end. Check that the content you needed — the booking reference, the order total, the full article — is present and not cut off, and that images loaded rather than showing as empty frames.

For receipts and confirmations, check the important numbers specifically: the date, the amount and any reference code. A PDF that captured the page header but lost the table with the total is not much of a record.

Name the file for what it is — the merchant, the date and what it records — and keep it somewhere you will find it. If you are collecting several saved pages, such as a month of receipts, Merge PDF can combine them into one tidy document.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a converter to save a webpage as a PDF?

No. Every major browser can save the page you are viewing as a PDF from its print dialog. A converter helps when you have a saved page file or HTML code rather than a live page.

Why are images missing from my saved PDF?

Many sites load images only as you scroll. Scroll to the bottom of the page before saving so every image has loaded, then save again.

Can PDFNest convert a web address to PDF?

No. PDFNest converts HTML files and pasted code in your browser and does not fetch pages from other websites. For a live page, use your browser’s Save as PDF.
